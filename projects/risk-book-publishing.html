Risk Management - book publishing industry

Written Analysis Risk management

Scope

Industry

Global, national, and self-publishing book publishers — academic publishing excluded due to distinct risk profile

Risk categories

Operational, financial & market, technological, legal & IP, reputational & climate

Framework

Frequency–severity matrix, assessed against current industry practices and academic literature

Risk prioritisation matrix

Risks were positioned by how often they occur (frequency) and how damaging they are when they do (severity). The most critical quadrant — high frequency, high severity — demands active management; the low-low quadrant can be retained or monitored.

Key findings

The two biggest risks

Supply chain cost inflation and demand model risk sit at the top of the priority stack — they're strongly correlated with each other, and managing them well reduces exposure across several other risk categories simultaneously.

Where practices fall short

The industry overinvests in controlling medium-importance risks while underinvesting in forecasting. Current demand models rely on a narrow set of variables and don't capture important signals — publisher reputation, release season, distribution channel mix, or community-driven demand from platforms like Goodreads.

Recommendation

Integrate LLM-driven analytics and external demand signals into forecasting processes. Richer models reduce the probability of stock mismatches — but scenario-based contingency planning (agile reprinting, expanded outlet partnerships) remains essential alongside any model, since Power's (2004) critique still applies: no model assumes a stable future.

Reputational risk in Eastern European publishing

Own analysis

Overmanaging reputation risk creates a different, larger risk

National publishers in Eastern Europe systematically avoid books on taboo or controversial topics — LGBTQIA+ narratives, books by authors of colour, politically sensitive subjects — until after they become proven international hits. By that point, most readers have already bought or read the book in English from a global publisher.

The result: the reputational risk they're trying to avoid is real but manageable, while the risk they create — chronic overproduction driven by established demand that's already been captured elsewhere — is more costly and harder to recover from.

Longer term, this contributes to a generational shift in reading habits toward English-language books, which erodes the core competitive advantage that national publishers actually have: the ability to champion local authors writing in the native language of their market.
